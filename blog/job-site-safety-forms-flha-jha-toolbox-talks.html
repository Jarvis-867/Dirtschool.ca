Job Site Safety Forms — FLHA, JHA, and Toolbox Talks Explained

April 2026  ·  9 min read  ·  Dirt School

If you're new to foreman work or running your own small crew, the paperwork side of site safety can feel like a bureaucratic mess. But these three forms — the FLHA, the JHA, and the toolbox talk — exist for a reason. Done right, they protect your crew, protect you legally, and occasionally save someone's life.

Here's what each one actually is, what goes in it, and how to use them without turning every morning into a two-hour meeting.

FLHA — Field Level Hazard Assessment

What it is

A Field Level Hazard Assessment is a quick, on-the-spot hazard check done before starting any new task — or any time conditions change. It takes 5–10 minutes and covers the specific hazards present in the immediate work area right now.

The FLHA is different from a full Job Hazard Analysis. It's not done in an office the week before. It's done at the tailgate, at the start of the shift, by the people about to do the work.

Who completes it: The crew doing the work. Everyone must review and sign. The foreman leads it, but every worker on site is responsible for the hazards they see.

What goes in an FLHA

  • Date, time, weather, temperature
  • Task description — what exactly are you about to do
  • Hazard categories — traffic, overhead lines, underground utilities, excavation, pinch points, weather, WHMIS materials, fire
  • For each hazard: Is it present? What's the risk level (High/Medium/Low)? What are the controls?
  • PPE required — hard hat, vest, safety glasses, hearing protection, etc.
  • Crew signatures — everyone signs before work starts

When to redo the FLHA

Any time conditions change significantly — new task, different work area, weather change, new hazard discovered. The FLHA isn't a one-and-done for the whole day.

Legal reality: If there's an incident and you can't produce a signed FLHA, you and your company are exposed. The form is your documentation that you identified and addressed hazards. Keep them on site and file them.

JHA — Job Hazard Analysis

What it is

A Job Hazard Analysis breaks a complex task down into individual steps, identifies the hazard at each step, rates the risk, and documents the controls. It's done before starting a high-risk job — not at the tailgate in the morning, but in advance by the supervisor or safety officer.

JHA structure

  1. Job / Task: what specifically is being done
  2. Personnel involved: who's on this task
  3. Required PPE: specific to this job
  4. Step-by-step breakdown: break the task into 5–10 sequential steps
  5. For each step: identify the hazard, rate the risk (H/M/L), list the control measures
  6. Signatures: prepared by, reviewed by

Risk rating — H/M/L

Use a simple matrix: Likelihood × Severity = Risk. High risk = stop work, escalate, and get senior approval before proceeding. Medium = manage with controls. Low = standard precautions.

When you need a JHA

  • Any non-routine or unfamiliar task
  • Work near energized equipment or live utilities
  • Excavation over 1.2m (4 ft) deep
  • Lifting operations or crane work
  • Any task with a history of incidents in your industry
  • When the client or prime contractor requires it

Toolbox Talks

What they are

A toolbox talk is a short, focused safety discussion — 5 to 15 minutes, one topic, done at the start of a shift or before a specific task. They're not lectures. The best ones are conversations.

How to run one that people actually pay attention to

  1. Pick one topic. One. Not "safety in general."
  2. Make it relevant to today's work. A toolbox talk about electrical hazards on a grading-only day doesn't land.
  3. Start with a question — "Has anyone here seen someone get hurt doing this?" People engage when it's real.
  4. Key points only — 3 to 5 max. Not a safety manual reading session.
  5. Discussion question at the end — "How does this apply to our site today?"
  6. Record attendance. Everyone who was there signs the sheet.

20 topics worth covering

  • Working around heavy equipment — exclusion zones, eye contact, spotters
  • Ground disturbance and utility locates
  • PPE — using it right, inspecting it, replacing it
  • Slip, trip, and fall prevention
  • Fatigue and fitness for work
  • Heat stress and working in the heat
  • Fueling and fluid handling
  • Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)
  • Machine pre-shift inspections
  • Incident and near-miss reporting
  • Excavation and trench safety
  • Electrical safety — overhead and underground lines
  • Fire prevention and response
  • Spill prevention and environmental protection
  • Manual lifting and ergonomics
  • Working alone or in isolation
  • Cold weather safety
  • Communication on site — radio protocols, hand signals
  • Driving and traffic management
  • Mental health — construction workers and asking for help

Recording attendance

Every toolbox talk needs an attendance sheet. Date, topic, who led it, and every attendee's name and signature. Keep these on file — they're evidence that safety training happened.

Pre-Job Safety Meeting

Before mobilizing to a new site or starting a major new phase of work, a pre-job safety meeting covers the big picture: site orientation, emergency procedures, hazard identification, PPE requirements, traffic plan, utilities, and reporting procedures. Everyone on the crew attends and signs.

This is different from a daily toolbox talk — it's a full briefing on the site and the job. It should happen before any work begins.
