What is a Living Out Allowance (LOA) and How Much is it Worth?

April 2026 · 6 min read · For operators working pipeline, highway, and industrial projects in BC and Alberta

If you've ever been offered a camp or away job, you've heard the term LOA. Living out allowance. It gets tossed around a lot but most guys don't actually do the math on what it's worth. They should. A good LOA package can change the entire value of a job offer.

What is a Living Out Allowance?

A living out allowance is a daily payment your employer makes on top of your wages when you're working away from home. It's meant to cover the cost of accommodation and meals when you're camped out or staying in a town you don't live in.

It's not overtime. It's not a bonus. It's a separate daily allowance paid for every day you're on a rotation — including days off at camp. Most pipeline and industrial contracts in BC and Alberta pay LOA on all calendar days during a rotation, not just work days.

Typical rates right now: $150–165/day on pipeline spreads in BC. Northern BC and Yukon rates vary by contract. Some highway jobs have it, some don't. Check your offer letter or collective agreement before you commit.

Why LOA Matters More Than You Think

Here's the part most guys miss: LOA is tax-free.

CRA treats it as a reimbursement for living expenses, not employment income — as long as the rates are reasonable and you're genuinely working away from home. That means every dollar of LOA lands in your pocket at full value.

Run the numbers:

  • $165/day × 17 days on rotation = $2,805 tax-free
  • At a 40% effective tax rate, you'd need $4,675 in taxable wages to take home that same $2,805

That's the real value of LOA. It's not just $2,805. It's the equivalent of $4,675 in wages. On a month with two rotations, that's roughly $9,000 in taxable wage equivalent — all on top of your hourly rate.

A $2/hr wage increase sounds better than it is. LOA on a pipeline rotation is usually worth more than a $5/hr raise — and it doesn't get taxed.

Some employers provide a camp (room and board included) instead of paying LOA. In that case you're not getting the cash but your living expenses are covered. Either way — ask. Know what you're getting before you say yes to a job.

Who Pays It and When

The contractor pays LOA — not the union, not the client. Whether you get it depends on a few things:

  • Distance from your home address (usually triggered at 50–100 km away)
  • Your collective agreement or employment contract
  • Whether the employer is providing accommodation instead

If you're union, check your CA (collective agreement). If you're non-union, it's negotiated in your offer. Always ask upfront. "Does this job include LOA and at what rate?" is a completely normal question before accepting.

How to Calculate Your Real Total Comp

Stop thinking about jobs as just an hourly rate. Your real earnings are:

Total Comp = Base Pay + Overtime + LOA

Here's a worked example. 17 days on a pipeline spread:

  • Rate: $52/hr, 10-hour shifts
  • Regular hours: 8 hrs/day × 17 days = 136 hrs × $52 = $7,072
  • OT hours: 2 hrs/day × 17 days = 34 hrs × $78 (1.5×) = $2,652
  • LOA: $165/day × 17 days = $2,805 (tax-free)
  • Total gross: $9,724 + $2,805 tax-free

Compare that to a local job at $55/hr with no LOA and 8-hour days. You'd gross $7,480 for the same 17 days. The pipeline job pays significantly more even though the hourly rate is lower.

That's why you always run the full math before comparing job offers.

Always Ask Before You Accept

LOA rates are negotiated, not standardized. Even on union jobs the rate can vary depending on the collective agreement covering that specific project. Before you accept a camp or away rotation:

  • Is LOA included? Or is camp provided instead?
  • What's the daily rate?
  • Is it paid on days off at camp, or only work days?
  • What's the trigger distance from your home?

Get it in writing. Verbal commitments on LOA don't hold up when there's a dispute.
