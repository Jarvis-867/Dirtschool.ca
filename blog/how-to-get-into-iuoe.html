How to Get Into the IUOE — The Complete Guide from Application to First Dispatch

Published May 4, 2026 · 10 min read

If you've been working construction or running iron and you're tired of watching union operators on your site pull double your hourly rate, this guide walks through exactly how to make the jump. From figuring out which Local to apply to, through the aptitude test, the apprenticeship, and your first dispatch — what it actually looks like, what it costs, and how long it takes.

This is the Canada-wide version. If you're specifically applying to BC's Local 115, see How to Get Into the IUOE in BC for the local detail.

What the IUOE Actually Is

The International Union of Operating Engineers (IUOE) represents heavy equipment operators, stationary engineers, and surveyors across North America. It's been around since 1896. In Canada, it has roughly 50,000 members spread across regional Locals.

What the union does, in plain terms:

  • Negotiates wages. Collective agreements lock in your hourly rate, overtime rules, and per-diems. Contractors can't pay you less than scale.
  • Manages benefits. Drug, dental, vision, sometimes mental health, all on the union plan.
  • Runs the pension. Defined-benefit pension funded by employer contributions. This is the single biggest financial advantage of union membership.
  • Operates the dispatch hall. When work is available, the union sends members out before non-members. When work slows, the hall keeps you informed about what's coming up.
  • Provides training. Apprenticeship programs, certification courses, refresher training — all coordinated through the Local.

The trade-off: you pay union dues (typically 2–3% of gross wages) and you work under collective agreement rules. For most operators, the math works strongly in favour of joining.

Which IUOE Local Do You Apply To?

Canada has multiple IUOE Locals, each covering a specific region. You apply to the Local that covers where you'll be working.

Local Region Covered Approx. Members
Local 115 British Columbia + Yukon ~14,000
Local 955 Alberta ~5,000
Local 793 Ontario ~17,000
Local 904 Atlantic Canada (NS, NB, PEI, NL) ~2,500
Local 870 Manitoba ~1,500
Local 870 (Sask. unit) Saskatchewan ~1,200

A few things to know:

  • You can only apply to one Local at a time. Pick the one where you live or where you plan to work.
  • Reciprocity exists between most Locals. Once you're in, you can travel to work in other Locals' jurisdictions through reciprocal agreements. You don't have to re-apply.
  • Different Locals have different specialties. 955 is heavy on oil sands and pipeline. 115 covers everything from highway construction to mining. 793 is dominated by GTA and southern Ontario commercial construction.

Step-by-Step: The Application Process

The path is similar across most Locals, with small variations:

Step 1: Decide which Local and which trade

The IUOE represents multiple classifications. Most readers of this guide want Heavy Equipment Operator (HEO), but the union also covers crane operators, surveyors, and stationary engineers. The application is different for each.

For HEO, you're applying to the apprenticeship program in your region.

Step 2: Fill out the apprenticeship application

Each Local has its own application portal:

  • Local 115 (BC/Yukon): iuoe115.ca
  • Local 955 (Alberta): iuoe955.com
  • Local 793 (Ontario): iuoelocal793.org
  • Local 904 (Atlantic): iuoelocal904.ca

You'll need:

  • Government-issued ID
  • High school diploma or equivalent (most Locals require this)
  • Driver's licence (Class 5 minimum, Class 3+ helpful)
  • Work history (any construction, equipment, or trades experience helps)
  • Application fee — typically $50–150 depending on the Local

Step 3: Wait for the aptitude test invitation

After your application is reviewed, you'll be scheduled for the aptitude test. Wait times vary by Local and by intake season — sometimes a few weeks, sometimes a few months.

This wait is your prep window. Use it.

Step 4: Take the aptitude test

The test format varies slightly by Local, but generally covers four sections:

  • Math — fractions, percentages, basic algebra, conversions, area/volume
  • Mechanical reasoning — gears, pulleys, levers, hydraulics, simple machines
  • Reading comprehension — passages with multiple-choice questions
  • Spatial reasoning — rotations, 3D visualization, diagrams

You typically have 2–3 hours. No calculator allowed. This catches a lot of operators off guard.

Step 5: Get your placement

After you pass, you're placed on a list ranked by score. Higher scores get earlier dispatch opportunities. Some Locals also factor in industry experience and other criteria.

⚠️ The Three-Attempts Rule (Read This Before You Sit Cold)

Here's what most guides bury at the bottom: most IUOE Locals limit you to three aptitude test attempts. Fail all three and you're permanently barred from reapplying through that Local.

This is not a rumour. It's in the application rules. There's no appeal.

What this means in practice: walking in cold to "see how it goes" is the worst possible decision you can make. You burn one of three lifetime attempts for no benefit.

The math:

  • Cost of preparing properly (free practice tests + a $29 study guide + 2–3 weeks of focused study): ~$30 and 30 hours of your time
  • Cost of failing all three attempts: a closed door on a career that pays $1.5–2 million more than non-union work over a 25-year span

Sit when you're ready. Not before.

What the Apprenticeship Actually Looks Like

If you pass the aptitude test, you're placed on the apprenticeship dispatch list. From there, the union sends you to signatory contractors as work comes up.

The apprenticeship is competency-based in most provinces, meaning you progress by accumulating hours on specific equipment, getting signed off by journeymen, and completing technical training periods.

Typical Timeline

  • Years 1–2: Lower-tier equipment — compactors, skid steers, smaller loaders. Learning grade stakes, jobsite communication, daily inspections, the basics. Pay starts at ~60% of journeyman scale.
  • Years 2–3: Larger equipment under supervision — excavators, dozers, motor graders. More responsibility. ~70–80% of scale.
  • Years 3–4: Solo operation of major equipment, more complex work. ~80–90% of scale.
  • Year 4–5: Journeyman ticket. 100% of scale plus full benefits and pension contributions.

The actual time varies. Some apprentices finish in 3 years if they're getting steady work. Others stretch to 5+ if work is slow or they take time off. The hour requirements are absolute — you can't skip them — so it depends on how fast you accumulate them.

Technical Training Periods

Each year of apprenticeship typically includes a few weeks of in-classroom or training-centre time. This is paid through EI in most provinces while you attend. Topics covered:

  • Equipment-specific operation (manufacturer training on Cat, Komatsu, etc.)
  • Safety certifications (WHMIS, fall arrest, confined space, H2S Alive for some specialties)
  • Surveying basics, blueprint reading, grade calculations
  • Trade math — applied to real construction scenarios

Your First Dispatch — What to Expect

Your first call from the hall is a moment most operators remember. Here's what typically happens:

  • Phone call from dispatch. They'll tell you the contractor, the project, the start date, the location, and which equipment you'll be on.
  • You confirm or decline. Declining without good reason can affect your dispatch priority. Going on a project you can't actually do can affect your reputation. Use judgment.
  • You report to site. Show up early. Hi-vis, steel-toes, hard hat, gloves. Bring your Local card, ID, and any required certifications.
  • You meet the foreman. They'll show you the machine, the work area, the daily routine. Ask questions early — it's expected for new apprentices.
  • You start the work. Don't try to be a hero. First-day operators who try to show off get noticed for the wrong reasons. Work safe, work steady, ask when unsure.

Common Reasons People Don't Get In

After watching this process for a long time, here's why people fall out:

  • They sit the aptitude test cold. Burns 1 of 3 attempts for no reason. Most common preventable failure.
  • They don't have a Class 3+ licence and don't want to get one. Most operator work requires moving between sites. No Class 3 = limited dispatch.
  • They have a criminal record they don't disclose. Most Locals check. Honesty about a past charge is fine. Lying about it ends the application.
  • They're not flexible on location. "I'll only work within 30 minutes of home" closes most of the dispatch options. Work goes where the work is.
  • They expect to be a journeyman immediately. Coming in with attitude or expecting senior pay/treatment as a 1st-year apprentice gets noticed fast.

None of these are dealbreakers if you avoid them. They're all preventable.

The Honest Truth

The union path isn't for everyone. Some operators prefer non-union work — fewer rules, more direct pay, easier to leave. That's a legitimate choice.

But if you want the highest-paying, most secure version of this trade, the union is the path. The aptitude test is the gate. And the aptitude test is the most preventable failure point in the entire process.

Most operators who fail it failed because they walked in cold. The fix is straightforward: free practice tests, a study guide, three weeks of focused prep. Then walk in ready.
